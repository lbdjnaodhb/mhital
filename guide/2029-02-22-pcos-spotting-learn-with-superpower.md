You're spotting between periods and you're not sure if it's your PCOS or something else. Irregular bleeding is one of the most common PCOS symptoms, but it's also one of the most anxiety-inducing because the causes range from harmless hormonal fluctuations to something that warrants medical attention.

What happens in the uterus when you have PCOS

In a typical menstrual cycle, estrogen builds the uterine lining during the first half of the cycle, and progesterone stabilizes it after ovulation. When progesterone drops at the end of the cycle, the lining sheds in an organized way, producing a predictable period. With PCOS, this process breaks down. Without ovulation, the corpus luteum doesn't form, and progesterone production remains low or absent. Estrogen continues to stimulate the endometrium without the counterbalancing effect of progesterone, causing the lining to thicken unevenly.

Eventually, the lining becomes unstable and sheds in an irregular, unpredictable manner. The endometrium isn't shedding all at once in response to a hormonal signal; instead, it's breaking down in fragments as it outgrows its blood supply. This is what causes spotting between periods or prolonged episodes of light bleeding that don't resemble a normal menstrual flow.

How hormonal imbalance drives irregular bleeding

Estrogen dominance and endometrial overgrowth

Without progesterone to regulate endometrial growth, estrogen stimulates continuous thickening of the uterine lining. The result is spotting that occurs at unpredictable intervals, sometimes light and sometimes heavier, depending on how much tissue has accumulated.

Anovulatory cycles and breakthrough bleeding

Without ovulation, there's no progesterone surge to organize the shedding of the endometrium. Instead, the lining continues to thicken until it becomes mechanically unstable, leading to breakthrough bleeding. This type of bleeding can be light and intermittent, or it can present as a prolonged, irregular flow that doesn't follow a predictable cycle.

Insulin resistance and androgen excess

Insulin resistance, present in many women with PCOS, amplifies hormonal dysfunction. Elevated insulin levels increase ovarian androgen production, which further disrupts normal follicle development and ovulation. High androgens also interfere with the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, perpetuating anovulation and irregular bleeding patterns.

Why some women experience heavy bleeding and clots

While some women with PCOS experience light spotting, others have the opposite problem: heavy periods with large blood clots. This happens when the endometrium has been building for an extended period without shedding. When it finally does shed, the volume of tissue is substantial, and the bleeding can be rapid and heavy. In a normal period, enzymes break down clotting factors to keep menstrual blood liquid. But when the flow is heavy and fast, clots form before these enzymes can act. Larger clots, especially those bigger than a quarter, can indicate significant endometrial buildup and warrant medical evaluation.

Heavy bleeding episodes in PCOS are often preceded by months of amenorrhea or light spotting. The longer the interval between periods, the thicker the lining becomes, and the heavier the eventual bleed.

Why bleeding patterns vary between women with PCOS

Not all women with PCOS experience the same bleeding patterns. Genetic differences influence how sensitive the endometrium is to estrogen and how efficiently the body metabolizes hormones. Some women have more robust progesterone production even without full ovulation, which can reduce the severity of spotting. Others have more pronounced insulin resistance or higher androgen levels, which worsen anovulation and bleeding irregularities.

Prior use of hormonal contraceptives can also affect bleeding patterns. Women who have been on birth control pills may experience more regular cycles initially after stopping, but anovulation often returns over time if PCOS is not managed. Conversely, women who have never used hormonal contraception may have more erratic patterns from the outset.

Thyroid function plays a role as well. Hypothyroidism, which is more common in women with PCOS, can worsen menstrual irregularities and contribute to heavier bleeding. Addressing thyroid dysfunction can sometimes improve cycle regularity even without directly treating PCOS.

When spotting signals something more serious

Persistent spotting or prolonged periods of amenorrhea followed by heavy bleeding can indicate endometrial hyperplasia, a condition where the uterine lining becomes abnormally thick. Left untreated, endometrial hyperplasia can progress to atypical hyperplasia, which carries a higher risk of endometrial cancer. Women with PCOS have a 2.7-fold increased risk of endometrial cancer compared to the general population.

Heavy bleeding that soaks through a pad or tampon every hour for several hours, or bleeding that lasts longer than seven days, should be evaluated. This level of blood loss can lead to anemia and may require medical intervention to stop the bleeding and assess the endometrium.

Postmenopausal bleeding or bleeding after a prolonged period of amenorrhea (more than six months) should always be investigated. While anovulation is common in PCOS, extended periods without shedding the endometrium increase the risk of hyperplasia and require assessment with imaging or biopsy.

If spotting is accompanied by pelvic pain, fever, or unusual discharge, it may indicate an infection or other structural issue unrelated to PCOS. These symptoms require prompt medical attention.

Using biomarkers to understand your bleeding patterns

Tracking hormonal and metabolic markers over time provides insight into what's driving your bleeding patterns and whether interventions are working. Key biomarkers include estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, luteinizing hormone, and follicle-stimulating hormone.

Insulin and glucose markers, including fasting insulin, hemoglobin A1c, and fasting glucose, help assess insulin resistance, which directly impacts ovulation and bleeding regularity. Improving insulin sensitivity through diet, exercise, or medication like metformin can restore more regular cycles in some women.

Thyroid markers, including TSH, free T4, and thyroid antibodies, should be checked if bleeding patterns worsen or if other symptoms of hypothyroidism are present. Correcting thyroid dysfunction can improve menstrual regularity and reduce heavy bleeding.

Inflammatory markers like high-sensitivity C-reactive protein can reflect underlying metabolic dysfunction that contributes to hormonal imbalance. Chronic inflammation worsens insulin resistance and can perpetuate anovulation.

Tracking these markers over time, rather than relying on a single snapshot, reveals trends and helps distinguish between normal fluctuations and patterns that require intervention.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is spotting between periods normal with PCOS?

Yes, spotting between periods is common in PCOS and usually results from anovulation and low progesterone. The uterine lining sheds irregularly when it's not stabilized by progesterone after ovulation. While it's common, persistent or heavy spotting should be evaluated to rule out endometrial hyperplasia.

Why do I have large blood clots during my period with PCOS?

Large blood clots form when the endometrium has thickened over an extended period without shedding, and then sheds rapidly. The body's natural anticoagulants can't keep up with the heavy flow, so clots form. Clots larger than a quarter or accompanied by very heavy bleeding warrant medical evaluation.

Can PCOS cause bleeding after months without a period?

Yes. When you don't ovulate for months, the uterine lining continues to thicken under estrogen stimulation. Eventually, it becomes unstable and sheds, often resulting in heavy, prolonged bleeding. This pattern is typical of anovulatory PCOS and increases the risk of endometrial hyperplasia if it happens repeatedly.

How do I know if my spotting is dangerous?

Spotting that occurs daily for weeks, bleeding that soaks through a pad every hour for several hours, or bleeding after six or more months of amenorrhea should be evaluated. These patterns can indicate endometrial hyperplasia or other complications that require imaging or biopsy.

Does treating insulin resistance help with irregular bleeding?

Yes. Improving insulin sensitivity can restore more regular ovulation in some women with PCOS, which leads to more predictable periods and less spotting. Interventions include dietary changes, exercise, and medications like metformin, inositol supplements, or insulin-sensitizing supplements such as berberine.

Can stress make PCOS spotting worse?

Yes. Chronic stress elevates cortisol, which can suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis and worsen anovulation. This can trigger spotting or delay periods further. Managing stress through sleep, movement, and nervous system regulation can improve cycle regularity over time.

Should I track my bleeding patterns if I have PCOS?

Yes. Tracking the timing, duration, and heaviness of bleeding helps you and your provider identify patterns and assess whether interventions are working. It also helps distinguish normal variation from concerning changes that require further evaluation.

What tests should I get if I have persistent spotting with PCOS?

A pelvic ultrasound can assess endometrial thickness and rule out structural abnormalities. Hormonal testing, including estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, LH, and FSH, helps clarify the underlying hormonal imbalance. If the endometrium is thickened, an endometrial biopsy may be recommended to rule out hyperplasia.
