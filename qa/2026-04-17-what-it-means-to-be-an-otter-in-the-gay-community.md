# What It Means To Be An 'Otter' In The Gay Community

THERE IS A whole animal kingdom within the gay male community. Many queer men (proudly) identify as bears, cubs, wolves, giraffes, bulls, chickens—all terms used to describe their appearance. The term to describe a man who has a slim to average build, with body and facial hair, and a more masculine appearance is called an otter.

What exactly is an otter?

“Otters belong to the broader bear community [plus-sized, hairy gay men] and are celebrated for their unique combination of leanness and body hair,” says Lee Phillips, LSCW, a psychotherapist and sex therapist who often works with LGBTQIA+ populations. “They are not as large as bears, but also not as hairless as twinks [thin, hairless gay men].”

The origins of the term otter aren’t entirely clear. “I don’t think there’s any one moment or person who defined this term,” says Joe Kort, PhD, a psychotherapist and board-certified clinical sexologist. However, we do have a sense of when it emerged: “The term otter appeared in the 80s and 90s within the bear subculture,” Phillips explains. “Otter gained popularity through gay magazines, online forums, and dating apps. The rise of social media and pop culture references helped elevate the term into broader queer awareness.”

The definition has shifted somewhat over the past few decades, Kort notes. “In the early days, an otter didn’t necessarily have to have body hair—just facial hair. Now, there’s an expectation of body hair, too.”

Why the term matters

While “otter” might sound like a playful, simple self-description, it often plays a vital role in a queer man’s identity. “It provides a sense of belonging, which can be hard to find in the gay male community because of the many different cliques and subcultures, and the often exclusive nature of those groups,” Kort says. The otter community is known for being friendlier and less judgmental than other subcultures within the gay community.

Identifying as an otter is also a way to embrace your natural body. “Body positivity is especially challenging for many, but in the gay male community, it’s compounded by the visible ideal of men in shape, and the stereotype that we should always be fit,” Kort adds. “The otter, while slim and lean, isn’t muscular, offering a different perspective on what attractiveness and body positivity can look like.”

Benjamin Camras, known as the “Flirt Coach,” recalls the journey of embracing his body hair. “I wasn’t exactly made fun of earlier in life, but I could tell people thought it was gross and didn’t want to be around me,” he says. “Growing up in the 90s and early 2000s, there weren’t many guys with body hair on TV, in movies, or on magazine covers.” Most men portrayed in the media had hairless bodies and chiseled muscles—Camras didn’t fit that mold. “I’m glad times are changing, but most leading men (in all media) are still mostly hairless.”

That’s why embracing the otter identity can be so impactful— it can give queer individuals a sense of belonging and pride, Phillips explains. “It creates a space for sexual and emotional validation, often within bear or otter-focused communities that promote acceptance and a sense of chosen family.”
