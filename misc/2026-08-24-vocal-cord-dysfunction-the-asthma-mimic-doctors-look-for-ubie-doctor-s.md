Vocal Cord Dysfunction: The Asthma Mimic Doctors Look For

Vocal cord dysfunction (VCD) occurs when the vocal cords close instead of opening during inhalation, creating symptoms that closely resemble asthma. Misdiagnosis is common, but distinguishing VCD from asthma is crucial for effective treatment and improved quality of life.

What Is Vocal Cord Dysfunction?

Definition: A temporary narrowing of the vocal cords during breathing in, causing airflow obstruction at the throat.

How It Differs from Asthma: In asthma, the lower airway (bronchi) narrows; in VCD, the upper airway (vocal cords) closes.

Prevalence: Often under-recognized; may affect athletes, singers, teens, and people with reflux disease.

Common Triggers and Risk Factors

Vocal cord dysfunction can arise from a mix of physical and emotional factors:

Smoke, strong odors, cleaning products

Cold air or pollution

Acid reflux can irritate the vocal cords

Particularly in cold or dry environments

Viral or bacterial infections can inflame the larynx

Anxiety or emotional tension may contribute

Recognizing the Symptoms

Symptoms of vocal cord dysfunction often overlap with asthma, but key differences help guide diagnosis:

Sudden onset, often during exercise or stress

Short episodes (seconds to minutes)

High-pitched "stridor" or wheezing on inhalation

Throat tightness or choking feeling

Little or no improvement with inhaled asthma medications

Voice changes (hoarseness)

Frequent throat clearing

How Vocal Cord Dysfunction Is Diagnosed

Accurate diagnosis of vocal cord dysfunction typically involves:

Detailed account of breathing difficulties, triggers, and previous asthma diagnosis

Listening for stridor vs. wheezing

Measures airflow patterns; inspiratory loop flattening suggests VCD

Flexible scope placed through the nose to visualize vocal cord movement

Methacholine challenge (to rule out asthma)

pH probe (to assess acid reflux)

Life doesn't have to be on hold. With proper management, most people can return to regular activities:

Maintain a symptom diary: note triggers, duration, and relief methods

Warm up your voice and breathing before exercise or public speaking

Avoid known irritants and control reflux through diet and sleep position

Stay hydrated to keep vocal cords lubricated

When to Seek Urgent Care

Although vocal cord dysfunction is not usually life-threatening, severe breathing difficulty should never be ignored. Seek immediate medical attention if you experience:

Rapid worsening of shortness of breath

Bluish tint to lips or fingertips

Confusion, lightheadedness, or extreme fatigue

For concerns that could be serious or life-threatening, speak to a doctor right away.
