MY SMARTWATCH constantly dings with cheerful congratulations for steps taken, heart rate achieved, days rested to speed up my recovery. My shelves, meanwhile, groan with helpful books, my phone with apps, and my counter with supplements that promise to remedy any deficiency that might inhibit my peak human performance. So why, if my life is so optimized, do I feel so…meh?

Maybe optimization—a trend that rose concurrently with our ability to track our body’s data—isn’t so optimal. We tend to overvalue what we can measure and undervalue the immeasurable. Yet much of what makes life meaningful and keeps us mentally healthy is not so easily captured in metrics. (See: There’s no tracking ring for contentment.) We worked with top mental wellness experts to ID what all our meters might be missing and learn how to, ugh, optimize it.

IT'S NOT you. The world has gotten louder. Emergency vehicle sirens are 30 percent louder than they were in 1912. Today, roughly 50 percent of the population is exposed to noise levels that could be dangerous to their health; excess noise can lead to hearing loss, cardiovascular disease, and cognitive impairment and can just plain put you on edge.

[Fixable Health Risks Your](https://incretin-path.escortskhi.com/blog/6023377388.html)

While turning down the exterior world’s volume is important to your life and mind, so is tempering your interior volume. We’re capable of self-talk at a compressed rate of 4,000 words a minute. (Experts have estimated we hear the equivalent of about 320 State of the Union addresses daily.) Turning that barrage down helps you to feel calmer and deal with the world more effectively—yet this is the most terrifying kind of silence to embrace, say Justin Zorn and Leigh Marz, authors of Golden: The Power of Silence in a World of Noise. According to a 2014 study in the journal Science, during a 15-minute period alone in a room with no external stimuli, 67 percent of men and 25 percent of women chose to administer a shock to themselves rather than sit with their thoughts.

“Silence is scary when we’re conditioned for novelty,” Zorn and Marz explain. So start small: Find a quiet place and sit. Try not to squirm or squiggle. Note your thoughts and let them go. Try it for five minutes. External silence and stillness help us hear our internal monologue. Hearing and recognizing our own thoughts is what allows us to be more mindful and aware of our surroundings, which can help reduce anxiety.

Friend Deficiency

“THE SINGLE best predictor of your psychological and physical health and well-being is the number and quality of close friends you have,” says Robin Dunbar, PhD, DSc, a professor emeritus of evolutionary psychology at Oxford University and coauthor of The Social Brain: The Psychology of Successful Groups. Spending time with friends releases endorphins—opioid neuropeptides that soothe and delight us.

Dunbar pinpointed the ideal number of what he calls “shoulders to cry on” friends, the close friends you need for well-being. “We did a big study of the predictors of depression in the future across about 13 European countries,” he says. Having five close friends minimized the likelihood of depression symptoms over two years. Interestingly, so did having three leisure activities that you regularly do. A mixture is fine; just don’t try to max out both—five close friends plus three activities. He warns that you’ll stretch yourself too thin and not be able to devote sufficient free time to cultivating those friendships.

Grief Deficiency

YES, IT'S strange to suggest that more yearning, sadness, or sense of loss can lead to better mental health. But grief happens whether you want it to or not, and when you do feel it, it’s important to not shut it down. Grief is a form of learning, explains neuroscientist Mary-Frances O’Connor, PhD, author of The Grieving Body. Staying open to the emotion helps you find that you can endure it; you discover how to be in a world in which something or someone you loved no longer exists, she says.

Letting yourself feel grief when it arises rather than pushing it away can help you better access skills like acceptance and emotional regulation, O’Connor says. Importantly, grief is not just grief. “Grief and joy operate on the same emotional channel,” she adds. “You can’t feel one if you can’t feel the other.”

Free Time Deficiency

THE STANDARD 40-hour workweek was adopted in the 1930s and hasn’t changed since. Over the past century or so, our free time—that is, time not spent at work—has remained relatively constant. This is the kind of time, some scientists say, that can help you be resilient to the stress life throws at you.

So why do we feel like we never have enough free time? That unsatisfied feeling comes not so much from how much free time we have but from what we do with it, says Gary S. Cross, PhD, a professor of modern history at Penn State and the author of Free Time: The History of an Elusive Ideal. “The question of free time is bound to the question of quality free time,” he says. “More free time that’s spent watching television can be relaxing, but it doesn’t bring the satisfaction that people have been expecting of free time for millennia.” Try instead “analog-ing on,” one of the top wellness trends predicted for 2025 at the Global Wellness Summit. Stow the screens and intentionally go IRL with something you’ve been wanting (not needing) to do—organize your vinyl, gather friends for a grill-off, or perfect your jump shot.
