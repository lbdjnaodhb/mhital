# Yes, 'Thunderstorm Asthma' Is a Thing — and It Affects More People Than You Think

DEPENDING ON WHO you ask, there are plenty of ways the weather can impact human health. People with knee arthritis say they feel stiffness when temperatures drop. Some claim that changes in air pressure can trigger migraines. Five percent of the US population experiences seasonal depression during months with fewer hours of sunlight. And now, there's a new one to add to the list: thunderstorm asthma.

The term refers to a phenomenon involving an increase in breathing issues during or immediately after a storm. In fact, it’s likely the reason behind a measured increase in ER cases, according to new research presented last week at the 2025 Annual Scientific Meeting of the American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology.

“I've had many patients come to me complaining that they experience worse breathing difficulties after a thunderstorm. She adds that it can be especially concerning for outdoor runners, who might mistake their breathing issues with fatigue.

How Do We Know It's Real?

From January 2020 to December 2024, researchers looked into sudden surges of asthma-related ER visits across three Kansas hospitals. An unusual pattern emerged from 627 (14%) of the 4,439 cases—it had stormed earlier in the day.

That number was disproportionately high. Records confirmed 38 thunderstorm days in the four-year period—or only 2 percent of the total measured days. On days when there was a storm, the average number of asthma-related ER visits was nearly 18, compared to only 3 cases a day when it wasn't storming. Researchers also found that on these days, older adults had an increased risk of being among those in the ER for breathing-related issues.

“These results confirm that thunderstorms can pose a serious health risk for people with asthma, even in the United States,” says Diala Merheb, MD, internal medicine resident at the University of Kansas Medical Center and lead study author. “Because storms are unpredictable, patients and health care providers should include storm-specific precautions in asthma action plans.”

What Causes Thunderstorm Asthma?

It's worth noting that the phrase is a bit misleading because its effects extend beyond asthma—many people with seasonal allergic rhinitis (AKA hay fever) are impacted. Research has estimated about 65 percent of people with seasonal allergies have dealt with thunderstorm asthma and nearly half had symptoms so severe it required a trip to the hospital.

Like hay fever, the culprit is ultimately pollen. While rain normally washes away pollen, that's not always the case when there's thunder and lightning involved.

“Wind, humidity, and lightning combined in a thunderstorm can cause the pollen to fall apart into much smaller pieces than usual. This makes the pollen lightweight enough to penetrate the deepest parts of the lungs when we breathe,” Lin explains. “For people who have asthma or a pollen allergy, these particles can lead to inflammation of the airways, as well as bronchospasm, where the airways constrict, making breathing difficult.”

Even if you don’t normally experience sneezing or a runny nose, Lin says people with allergies are often among those who experience shortness of breath or coughing during a thunderstorm.

What Does This Mean for Runners?

This increased pollen count doesn’t disappear after the storm passes, either.

If the weather remains wet, Lin says the tiny particles of pollen can linger in the air for a long time. “As we engage in exercise, we breathe harder, drawing in a higher volume of air, or higher volumes of pollen,” she explains.

If runners have a history of sensitive airways, hitting the pavement after a thunderstorm could keep the respiratory system hypersensitive for the rest of the day and increase the risk for uncontrollable coughing in the night. “I’ve worked with runners who did not associate shortness of breath or a tight chest after a summer run as possibly being due to exercise-induced bronchoconstriction that is already exacerbated by thunderstorm activity,” Lin says.

So, Should You Skip Your Post-Storm Run?

All of this doesn't necessarily mean you have to quit running outside after a storm if you have allergies or asthma. Instead, experts say to take proper precautions.

“It’s smart to think about thunderstorms the same way you prepare for high-pollen days or cold weather,” says Selina Gierer, DO, allergist at The University of Kansas Health System and coauthor of the new study. “Understanding triggers and having a clear plan can help avoid emergency room visits.”

Lin advises using weather sites or apps to check the pollen count and air quality before running. If pollen count is too high or there’s a possibility of a thunderstorm, it’s safer to stick to running indoors.

“Using your medications prior to running, staying hydrated, as well as monitoring your body's systems are small steps that greatly aid in managing asthma during running activities.”
