Explanation

High hematocrit levels can sound alarming, but understanding what they mean — and why they happen — can help you take the right next steps calmly and confidently.

Hematocrit refers to the percentage of your blood that is made up of red blood cells. Red blood cells carry oxygen throughout your body. When hematocrit levels are high, it means your blood contains a higher-than-normal concentration of red blood cells.

In some cases, this is temporary and easily corrected. In others, it may signal an underlying medical condition that needs attention. Let's break down the High Hematocrit symptoms, common causes (including dehydration), and when to speak with a doctor.

What Is Considered a High Hematocrit Level?

Normal hematocrit ranges vary slightly by age and sex, but generally:

Men: About 41% to 50%

Women: About 36% to 44%

A value above the upper limit may be considered elevated. However, a single high reading doesn't always mean something serious is wrong. Doctors usually look at trends, symptoms, and other lab values before making conclusions.

High Hematocrit Symptoms

Many people with mildly elevated hematocrit have no symptoms at all. When symptoms do occur, they are often related to thicker blood (increased blood viscosity), which can slow circulation.

Common High Hematocrit symptoms include:

Headaches

Dizziness or lightheadedness

Blurred vision

Fatigue

Flushing (reddish skin, especially on the face)

Shortness of breath

Itching (especially after a warm shower)

Numbness or tingling in hands or feet

High blood pressure

In more serious cases, high hematocrit levels can increase the risk of blood clots. Signs of a possible clot may include:

Sudden chest pain

Sudden shortness of breath

Swelling or pain in one leg

Sudden weakness or difficulty speaking

These symptoms require immediate medical attention.

The Link Between High Hematocrit and Dehydration

One of the most common and often overlooked causes of elevated hematocrit is dehydration.

How Dehydration Raises Hematocrit

When you're dehydrated, your body loses fluid — but not red blood cells. This means:

Plasma (the liquid portion of blood) decreases

Red blood cells become more concentrated

Hematocrit appears higher

This is called relative polycythemia, meaning the red blood cell count isn't actually increased — the blood is just more concentrated.

Common Causes of Dehydration Include:

Not drinking enough fluids

Excessive sweating

Vomiting or diarrhea

Fever

Intense exercise

Certain medications (like diuretics)

In these cases, correcting fluid balance often brings hematocrit levels back to normal.

Other Causes of High Hematocrit

While dehydration is common, it's not the only cause. Elevated hematocrit can also result from:

1. Living at High Altitude

At higher elevations, oxygen levels are lower. Your body compensates by producing more red blood cells to carry oxygen more efficiently.

2. Smoking

Smoking reduces oxygen delivery, which stimulates the body to make more red blood cells.

3. Chronic Lung or Heart Disease

Conditions that reduce oxygen levels in the blood can trigger increased red blood cell production.

4. Sleep Apnea

Repeated pauses in breathing during sleep can lower oxygen levels overnight, leading to higher hematocrit levels.

5. Polycythemia Vera

This is a rare bone marrow disorder in which the body produces too many red blood cells. It requires medical diagnosis and treatment.

6. Testosterone or Anabolic Steroid Use

These substances can stimulate red blood cell production.

Because the causes vary widely, proper medical evaluation is important if high levels persist.

When Should You Be Concerned?

A slightly elevated hematocrit with no symptoms — especially during an illness or dehydration episode — may not be dangerous.

However, you should speak to a doctor if:

Your hematocrit remains high on repeat testing

You have ongoing High Hematocrit symptoms

You experience symptoms of blood clots

You have known lung, heart, or sleep disorders

You feel persistently fatigued, dizzy, or short of breath

Persistently thickened blood can increase the risk of:

Blood clots

Stroke

Heart attack

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

How Doctors Evaluate High Hematocrit

If your hematocrit is elevated, your doctor may:

Repeat the blood test to confirm results

Review your hydration status

Check oxygen levels

Order additional blood tests (such as erythropoietin levels)

Test for sleep apnea if symptoms suggest it

Evaluate heart and lung function

Consider genetic testing for polycythemia vera if indicated

Diagnosis is based on both lab results and your overall health picture.

Managing High Hematocrit Levels

Treatment depends entirely on the cause.

If Dehydration Is the Cause:

Increase fluid intake

Address vomiting, diarrhea, or fever

Adjust medications if needed (under medical supervision)

If Related to Lifestyle:

Quit smoking

Improve hydration habits

Manage sleep apnea

Monitor altitude exposure

If Due to an Underlying Medical Condition:

Specific treatment may include medications

Therapeutic phlebotomy (removing blood) in certain disorders

Managing heart or lung disease

The key is identifying the underlying cause rather than focusing only on the lab number.

Practical Steps You Can Take Now

If you've recently been told your hematocrit is high:

✅ Drink adequate fluids daily

✅ Monitor for symptoms like dizziness or headaches

✅ Avoid smoking

✅ Follow up for repeat blood testing

✅ Keep track of new or worsening symptoms

Avoid self-diagnosing. Lab results need proper interpretation within the full clinical context.

A Balanced Perspective

It's natural to feel concerned when you see an abnormal lab result. But in many cases, high hematocrit levels are temporary — especially when linked to dehydration.

The important thing is not to ignore persistent symptoms or repeated abnormal results. High hematocrit symptoms can range from mild to serious depending on the cause.

If you experience:

Chest pain

One-sided weakness

Severe headache

Leg swelling or pain

Seek immediate medical care.

For anything ongoing, unclear, or potentially serious, speak to a doctor promptly. Only a qualified healthcare professional can properly evaluate your situation and determine whether further testing or treatment is necessary.

Final Thoughts

High hematocrit levels mean your blood contains a higher percentage of red blood cells. Sometimes this is simply due to dehydration. Other times, it may signal an underlying condition that needs medical attention.

Pay attention to High Hematocrit symptoms, stay well hydrated, and follow up with your healthcare provider if levels remain elevated.

Your health decisions should always be guided by accurate information and professional medical advice. If something feels serious or life-threatening, do not wait — speak to a doctor immediately.
